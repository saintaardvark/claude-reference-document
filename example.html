01 / The problemComparing two piles of data, point by point

The short version: you have samples from two distributions, p and q. For any point x, you want to know how much more likely it is under p than under q — on a log scale.

That quantity is the log relative density, log(p(x)/q(x)). Average it over p and you get one summary number: the KL divergence, a measure of how different the two piles are.

verified Relative density: Bach writes it dp/dq. Stated goal of the post: estimate its logarithm, which he says is equivalent to estimating KL.
Bach, "Relative density estimation as a testbed" section. francisbach.com
my reading KL divergence: zero when p = q, grows as they diverge. Not symmetric: KL(p‖q) ≠ KL(q‖p).

Why this shows up everywhere:

  • Classification. "How likely is class A given these features" is a ratio of densities in disguise.
  • Mutual information. Compare the joint distribution to the "if they were independent" distribution.
  • Normalising models. Turning arbitrary scores into probabilities that sum to 1 — softmax, the last layer of most neural nets.
  • Species distribution modelling. MaxEnt compares environments at presence records against background points.
verified Bach's "Mutual information" section: set q to the product of p's marginals, and the log-ratio becomes log p(x₂|x₁) − log p(x₂), i.e. conditional density estimation.
verified His intro lists normalising probabilistic models, smooth maxima, transformers and entropy-regularised RL. His conclusion names the neural-net last layer, where cross-entropy and log-sum-exp dominate, as a possible target.
verified Added, not requested. Elith et al. show MaxEnt minimises relative entropy (KL) between a density from presence data and one from the landscape; the model is effectively a log density ratio.
Elith, Phillips, Hastie et al. (2011). "A statistical explanation of MaxEnt for ecologists." Diversity and Distributions 17:43–57. abstract

The obstacle: averages of exponentials

The standard ("variational") recipe finds a scoring function v(x) by maximising an objective. That objective contains the average of ev(x) over samples from q.

Averages of exponentials are statistically nasty. A few rare samples with large v get exponentiated into enormous values and dominate the mean. Run it twice on fresh data and you get very different answers.

verified Two forms in the post. Eq. (1): Ep[v] + 1 − Eq[ev]. Eq. (2), Donsker–Varadhan: Ep[v] − log Eq[ev]. The second is the log-sum-exp form.
Bach, Eqs. (1)–(2). francisbach.com

Bach quantifies it. If the exponent is normally distributed with spread σ, the relative squared error of the average over n samples is:

(eσ² − 1) / n

More data helps only linearly; more spread hurts exponentially. At σ = 3 you need roughly 800,000 samples to get the relative squared error down to 1%. Taking a log afterwards doesn't fix it.

verified Formula from the post; also checked by simulation this session (σ = 0.5, 1, 2 matched closely).
my reading Arithmetic: e⁹ − 1 ≈ 8,100; 8,100 / 0.01 ≈ 810,000. At σ = 3 the simulation itself became unreliable — the heavy tail made even measuring the variance noisy, which is the point.

Bach's question, paraphrased: can you keep what's good about this log-sum-exp machinery while dodging its variance problem?

02 / The toolWhat least-squares is

Least-squares: choose parameters so that the sum of squared prediction errors is as small as possible.

For a model that is linear in its parameters, there is a closed-form answer — no iterative optimiser, no learning rate. You compute a mean vector and a covariance matrix from the data, then solve one linear system.

my reading Normal equations: θ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy. Everything needed is sums of products of the data — "moments" in Bach's language.

Why statisticians like it:

  • Stable. Only means and covariances are computed. No exponentials, so no rare-sample blow-ups.
  • Exact. Mature linear-algebra routines give the answer to machine precision.
  • Analysable. Its error behaviour is well understood.

Its known weakness: forcing it onto classification (regressing on 0/1 labels) causes artefacts, notably masking, where a class never wins anywhere.

verified Bach's own pros list: closed form via linear algebra, moments with fixed controlled variance, sharp analyses.
verified He cites Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, Elements of Statistical Learning §2.4, and shows masking in his final figure (top right: a class disappears).

Places you've likely met it:

  • Linear regression — sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression, Ridge, numpy.polyfit.
  • GIS georeferencing — fitting an affine or polynomial transform to ground control points.
  • GPS fixes and Kalman filters — position from over-determined, noisy measurements.
  • PCA, LDA, CCA — all reduce to eigenvalue problems on covariance matrices, the same machinery Bach uses in §03.
  • Density ratios already — uLSIF estimates p/q by least-squares.
unverified GDAL's gdalwarp -order N fits a polynomial from GCPs; I believe by least-squares but did not check the source.
unverified Standard textbook framing; not checked this session.
verified Python densratio package, default method="uLSIF". This corresponds to the Pearson divergence — the version Bach says has the wrong geometry.

03 / The trickWhy least-squares works here

The idea in one line: rewrite KL as a blend of simpler divergences, each of which is a least-squares problem, then solve the whole blend in one shot.

Step 1 — Split KL into a continuum of chi-square divergences

There is a family of "weighted chi-square" divergences, one for each dial setting ρ between 0 and 1. Bach's key identity says KL is exactly a weighted average of all of them.

You can take this identity on trust. It is the only genuinely clever piece of maths in the post.

verified ρ = 0 is Pearson chi-square; ρ = 1 is Neyman; ρ = ½ is Le Cam. Generator: f(t) = ½(t−1)²/(ρt + 1 − ρ).
verified t log t − t + 1 = ∫₀¹ (t−1)²(1−ρ)/(ρt+1−ρ) dρ. Checked numerically this session at t = 0.1, 1.5, 4, 20 — matches to machine precision. Bach says it comes from operator-convex function theory.

Step 2 — Each chi-square piece is a regression

For a fixed ρ, estimating that divergence is literally a least-squares regression:

  • Label every p sample with one target value, every q sample with another.
  • Fit a function of x to those labels by squared error.

The objective is quadratic, so only means and covariances appear. No exponentials → no exploding variance.

verified Targets: y = 1/ρ for p samples (weight ρ), y = −1/(1−ρ) for q samples (weight 1−ρ). Bach notes the resemblance to noise-contrastive estimation.

Step 3 — With linear features, each piece has a formula

Represent x by a feature vector φ(x) — raw coordinates, polynomials, random features, whatever. Then each ρ-regression has the closed form:

θ(ρ) = (ρ·Σp + (1−ρ)·Σq)⁻¹ (μp − μq)

Σ is a second-moment matrix, μ a mean. Four statistics of your data; that's all the method ever sees.

verified Transcribed from the "Linear models" section. Σp = Ep[φφᵀ], μp = Ep[φ].

Step 4 — One eigendecomposition solves every ρ at once

Problem: Step 3 needs a matrix inverse for every ρ in [0, 1], and the individual problems misbehave near 0 and 1.

Solution: a generalised eigendecomposition of the pair (Σp, Σq) finds a basis where both matrices are diagonal. In that basis every inverse is trivial, and the integral over ρ collapses to a finite sum:

KL estimate = Σᵢ ((μp−μq)ᵀvᵢ)² · f(λᵢ)/(λᵢ−1)²

The ρ = 0 / ρ = 1 instabilities cancel out in the sum.

my reading Bach draws the ridge-regression analogy himself (many penalty values, one decomposition). My gloss: each eigen-direction vᵢ is a direction where p and q have variance ratio λᵢ.
verified Condition he gives: stable whenever f(t)/(t−1)² stays bounded. For KL it's decreasing, starting at 1.

The price: bias for variance

The result is a lower bound on the true divergence — systematically a little low, but far less noisy.

  • Small or medium data: the variance saving wins; spectral beats variational.
  • Lots of data: variance shrinks anyway, so the unbiased variational method catches up or wins.
verified Bach's Gaussian analysis and 2-D simulations both show this crossover. My own run (5-D Gaussians, true KL 0.40, linear features) gave 0.51 → 0.45 → 0.38 → 0.35 at n = 50 → 20,000: converging to a value just under the truth.

A bonus Bach didn't plan for

Applied to labelled data, the same machinery gives a closed-form substitute for softmax / multinomial logistic regression. It behaves similarly to softmax. In his comparison figure, masking shows up in plain least-squares on one-hot labels, not in the KL spectral version.

verified His conclusion calls this an unintended consequence. Cost: one eigendecomposition, O(d²n + kd³), versus O(nd²k² + d³k³) per Newton iteration for softmax.

04 / In practiceWhen you'd use it, and what exists

Bottom line: there is no library switch for this yet. It is a May 2026 arXiv paper, due at NeurIPS; code ships as the paper's supplementary material. But the core is a few lines on top of SciPy.

verified Paper text: "code is provided as supplementary material". Searched for a public package or scikit-learn integration; found none.
Bach (2026). "A Spectral Framework for Closed-Form Relative Density Estimation." arXiv:2605.10668. arXiv

Reach for it when

  • You want a KL divergence or mutual information number from samples, and samples are limited relative to features.
  • You want the log density ratio itself — drift detection between two datasets, covariate-shift reweighting, presence-vs-background modelling.
  • You need a deterministic, non-iterative fit: no learning rate, no convergence checks, reproducible to machine precision.
  • You have a good feature map already (hand-built, random features, or a frozen network's embeddings).

Skip it when

  • Data is plentiful — plain logistic regression is as good and fully supported.
  • Raw data is high-dimensional with no good features; Bach warns KL estimation suffers the curse of dimensionality.
  • You need total variation distance — not covered by the framework.
  • Features number in the tens of thousands: cost is O(m²n + m³).
verified His simulations stay in 2-D for this reason; feature learning (an EM-like algorithm) is his proposed route to higher dimensions, with details deferred to a later post.

Existing tools, mapped to the article

  • The building block: scipy.linalg.eigh(Sp, Sq) solves the generalised problem Sp·v = λ·Sq·v with vᵀSq·v = 1 — exactly Bach's normalisation.
  • Same shape, different job: LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(solver="eigen") calls eigh(Sb, Sw) internally; RidgeCV(gcv_mode="eigen") is the "one decomposition, many penalties" trick.
  • The variational baseline: LogisticRegression trained to tell p-samples from q-samples; its log-odds estimate the log ratio (plus a constant from class sizes). Neural version: MINE.
  • Least-squares density ratio: densratio (uLSIF / RuLSIF / KLIEP) — the Pearson-divergence approach Bach improves on.
  • Different family entirely: sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_regression — nearest-neighbour MI estimate, no feature map.
  • Random features: sklearn.kernel_approximation.RBFSampler produces φ(x) you could feed in. Bach used ReLU random features instead.
  • MaxEnt SDMs: elapid.MaxentModel (Python) or dismo::maxent (R) — iterative, log-sum-exp based; a candidate for spectral comparison.
verified From SciPy 1.17 docstring, this session.
verified Read from scikit-learn 1.8 source this session: _RidgeGCV.fit decomposes X once, then loops over alphas reusing it.
my reading Follows from Bayes' rule; standard "classifier trick". Not a claim from the article.
verified MINE (Belghazi et al., ICML 2018) maximises the Donsker–Varadhan bound — Eq. (2), the exact form whose variance Bach targets. Its authors add a moving-average correction because the log-of-mean gradient is biased.
Belghazi et al. (2018). "Mutual Information Neural Estimation." ICML, PMLR 80:531–540. PMLR

Architecture, if you build it yourself

  • Featurise both sample sets with the same φ; include a constant column.
  • Moments: μp, μq, Σp, Σq. Add a small ridge term to Σq.
  • Decompose: eigh(Sp, Sq).
  • Sum the per-direction contributions with the KL weight f(λ)/(λ−1)², whose limit at λ = 1 is ½.

The last two steps, as tested this session:

lam, V = eigh(Sp, Sq)          # Sp v = lam Sq v
proj   = (mu_p - mu_q) @ V     # one score per direction
kl_hat = np.sum(proj**2 * w(lam))
# w(t) = (t*log t - t + 1)/(t-1)**2 ; w(1) = 0.5

Getting the potential v(x) — the per-point log ratio — needs the M, N, c matrices from the paper; that's the part worth reading it for.
